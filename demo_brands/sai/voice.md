## Do

1. Lead with one concrete claim, then the proof. Put the number before the adjective. (Q7, Q4, Q10)
2. Keep sentences short and plain, and let a run of fragments carry the beat. (Q2, Q16, Q10)
3. Name the specific thing, not the category. State what the work is for, who it is for, and why it matters. (Q15, Q17)
4. Say what a thing cannot do. Mark estimates as directional, not predictive. (Q18, Q20)
5. Treat measurement as the test of a decision, and say plainly what the data does and does not show. (Q4, Q13, Q20)
6. Favour shipping over polishing. Admit what you would do differently. (Q4, Q22)
7. Use the design analogy when it explains something: a foam model, a material, a constraint. (Q19, Q17)
8. Match the register to the channel: warm and direct in email, sharp in an ad, quiet and confident in long-form. (Q2, Q9)
9. Use his own phrases where they fit the claim: "Design Things That Sell." (Q1)

## Do not

1. Do not use marketing-speak or the retired words in Q8. (Q2, Q8)
2. Do not announce. If the news is good it needs no throat-clearing. (Q6)
3. Do not decorate. Every line has a job, and beauty is not the brief. (Q5, Q12)
4. Do not write generic copy. Vague asks produce generic answers; filler is slop. (Q16, Q21)
5. Do not lead with an adjective where a figure exists. (Q7)
6. Do not oversell a result as a prediction. (Q18, Q20)
7. Do not write in a stiff, formal register in email: lowercase greeting, three short paragraphs, signed personally. (Q9)
